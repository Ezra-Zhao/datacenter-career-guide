While that account is still to come, here is what usually happens. Most people react the same way on their first visit: it is too loud, too cold, and surprisingly dull.

It is loud because tens of thousands of fans are spinning at once. A server itself makes no sound; its fans do. One machine's fans are easy to ignore. Ten thousand machines lined up together produce a constant roar that presses on your ears, and after a while you have to raise your voice to be heard. It is cold because machines mind heat, not cold. A data hall holds its temperature, year round, in a range that suits the equipment and not the people in it. People add a layer when they go in; the machines do better with as little covering as possible. Stay a little longer and boredom sets in. There are no windows and no view — only row after row of identical black racks, their indicator lights blinking on and off. The film version — blue light everywhere, someone typing furiously in front of a giant screen — basically does not exist in real life. A real data center is like a warehouse when things are quiet, and like an emergency room when something goes wrong.

That gap is a good place to start understanding this industry. A data center is not a glamorous technology product. It is a factory that has to keep running 24 hours a day. Its quality is not in how it looks. It is in going ten years without an incident.

## 1.2 Three Shifts in Form

The term "data center" does not have a long history, but what goes inside one has changed three times.

The first form was the enterprise computer room era. From the end of the last century into the early years of this one, any company of a certain size had a computer room of its own: a few rooms, a batch of servers, looking after the company's email, finance systems, and internal websites. The IT department ran it. Scale was counted in machines. If the power went out, business stopped until it came back. In those days a server was a fixed asset, much like office desks and chairs — bought, used until it wore out, then scrapped.

Cloud computing drove the second shift. Around 2006, Amazon and others began selling computing power the way utilities sell water and electricity: you no longer had to buy machines, you could rent them. Once demand could be rented, supply was bound to concentrate. A small computer room cannot match the cost advantage of large-scale purchasing and large-scale operations, so computing power flowed out of thousands of enterprise computer rooms and into a small number of very large facilities. That is the cloud data center. Scale is now counted in megawatts (MW), and a single campus can hold as much as all the computer rooms of a city once did. Virtualization cut one physical machine into many "virtual machines" for different customers, and for the first time the machine was separated from the person using it. In this phase the industry learned to standardize: rack dimensions, power delivery, cooling design, and operating procedures all acquired a common language. That language is what Part Two of this book is about.

The third shift is happening now, and large-model training is driving it. After generative AI took off at the end of 2022, what runs inside data centers changed. Before, the work was mainly storing data and running applications — in essence, a warehouse plus an office. Now more and more facilities are doing something else: they take electricity and data in, and they put out trained models and the tokens produced by inference. During the 2024 GTC conference, NVIDIA Chief Executive Officer Jensen Huang called this kind of facility an "AI factory" outright. In his explanation to the media, the factories of the previous industrial revolution turned raw material into electricity; the factories of this round take data and electricity as their raw material, and their product is tokens (source: TechCrunch reporting on GTC 2024, 2024-03-19; paraphrase, with the short quotation recorded in the citation register).

That comparison is not just a figure of speech. It matches a real jump in the underlying numbers. A conventional rack draws a few kilowatts to just over ten kilowatts per rack. An AI training rack can reach several tens of kilowatts or more — the exact figure moves with each product generation, and Chapter 3 goes into it in detail. Facility design used to start with floor area. Now it starts with power: how many megawatts are available decides how much computing power can be installed. The way the industry talks has changed with it. Ask about a data center today and the first question is power, the second is land, and only then the building itself.

Three shifts, one thread running through them: computing power has moved from something every organization provided for itself to something produced centrally and taken on demand. Keep that thread in mind and every later chapter — power, cooling, delivery, operations, jobs — is answering the same question: once production is centralized, how do you make sure it never stops, never breaks off, and never goes wrong?

## 1.3 A Bird's-Eye View of a Data Center

Build a map in your head first. A typical data center divides, roughly, into two halves.

One half is the IT side — the part that does the work. Servers do the computing, storage equipment remembers, and network equipment moves things around. Compute, storage, and networking are the three basic parts of the IT side, and they are the subject of Chapters 6 and 7 of this book. They sit inside standard racks, standing in rows across the data hall. In the trade, that area is called the "white space," because data hall floors used to be mostly light in color.

The other half is the facility side — the part that keeps everything alive. The power distribution system takes utility power in and, step by step, transforms it, stabilizes it, and backs it up before delivering it to the power supply of every server. The cooling system carries the heat the servers give off out of the building. The fire protection system watches for smoke and flame. The security system controls access doors, cameras, and patrol checks. And there is a monitoring room, where the alarms from every system end up on the screens of the staff on duty. In floor area and in investment, the facility side is often no smaller than the IT side. Power distribution and cooling, the subjects of Chapters 4 and 5, are its two main arteries.

The relationship between the two halves is simple. The IT side decides how much a facility is worth. The facility side decides whether that value can drop to zero in a minute. If utility power fails and the UPS does not catch the load, and the diesel generator does not start, computing power worth hundreds of millions of dollars will shut down within minutes, and a model training job in progress may have to restart from a checkpoint saved hours or even days earlier. That is why, in data center design philosophy, reliability comes before performance. It is also why the Uptime Institute's Tier classification has become common language across the industry: it uses four grades, from Tier I to Tier IV, to describe how much redundancy a facility has built into its power and cooling paths, and whether maintenance can be carried out without shutting down (Chapter 4 sets out the classification in detail; only its published summary is used here, see the citation register for the source).

Keep this map. Every chapter that follows enlarges one part of it.

## 1.4 The Facilities Behind Every Tap

Bring the lens back to your own daily life.

In the morning you pick up your phone and watch a short video. That video sits on a storage array in some data center. The moment you tap it, it is read out and travels through the backbone network, the metropolitan network, and a base station before it reaches your screen. You send a friend a voice message, and somewhere you will never visit, it is stored and forwarded. You ask an AI assistant a question, and that question is sent into an AI factory, where a group of GPUs work on it together for a few seconds — when a large model cannot fit on a single card, several cards, or even dozens of them, have to work on it together — and the answer comes back to you, one token at a time.

These actions have one thing in common: none of them can wait. If a video stalls for three seconds, you swipe away. If an AI answer takes ten extra seconds, you close it. In engineering terms, "cannot wait" means this: the service has to stay on, a failure has to switch over in seconds, and data has to be backed up in more than one place. So facilities get larger, redundancy gets layered higher, and more people stand watch.

Demand only grows, and the reason is not complicated. Data is produced and, by default, not deleted. Photos, videos, chat records, surveillance footage, business reports — keeping them is the default choice; deleting them takes a deliberate decision. Computing is also getting "thicker." Watching the same short video involves far more calculation by the recommendation algorithm behind it than it did ten years ago. Asking a question and having AI generate an answer on the spot uses far more computing power than a traditional search flipping through an index. On top of that, new demand keeps being invented. Self-driving systems have to process data sent back from vehicles. Machine vision in factories has to inspect quality in real time. Drug research has to use computing power to screen molecules. Every time a new application is invented, a new batch of data halls has to stand ready for it.

This industry is not going to disappear. It will change shape — from computer room to cloud, and from cloud to AI factory — but as long as society keeps outsourcing its memory and its thinking to machines, someone will have to build these facilities and someone will have to watch over them. Most readers of this book are probably among those who want to become the people who watch over them.

Editorial outline (to be written up in this structure once the material arrives): start with an everyday comparison — for example, describing a data center as "a city's power plant and waterworks combined, except that what it produces is not electricity and water, but computing power." Then give an example the listener can check for themselves, such as "the photos stored on your phone live in a place like this." Then close with one plain truth from the author: the industry looks glamorous from the outside, but the daily work is inspection, record-keeping, part replacement, and standing watch — unglamorous, steady work. Being dependable matters more than knowing a lot.

At the end of the chapter, let us put it plainly. A data center is infrastructure for this era, the same kind of thing as the power grid, the water supply, and the transport network. Nobody thinks about it when it works. Everybody thinks about it when it fails. Anyone entering this field should accept that position first: the work you do is the work that holds things up from underneath. The credit is often invisible. The responsibility never is. Accept that, and the technical chapters that follow will be much easier to take in.
