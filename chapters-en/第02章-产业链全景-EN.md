## 2.1 Four Kinds of Players

From nothing to a working facility, a data center passes, roughly, through the hands of four kinds of players.

The first kind is the demand side, mainly cloud providers and large internet companies. Players at the level of Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta are both the largest builders and the largest users — they build campuses for their own cloud services and AI businesses, a model the trade calls hyperscale. What defines this kind of player is an enormous appetite for power and land: the total capacity of a single campus is often measured in hundreds of megawatts, and at the negotiating table they are forever the client among clients. The "cloud" that small and medium-sized businesses and ordinary developers use traces back, at its source, mostly to here.

The second kind is the colocation provider (colocation, or colo for short). They build the facility, then rent it out as a package of rack space, power, cooling, and network access, and customers move their own servers in. Colocation comes in two forms. Retail colocation sells by the rack, or even by the U (the unit of rack height); customers are many and orders are small, and the model wins on high unit prices and strong customer stickiness, with interconnection services — high-speed direct links between customers, and between customers and clouds — an important source of profit. Equinix is the representative of this model. Wholesale colocation sells by the megawatt: a single signing can cover a whole floor or a whole building, customers are few and orders are large, and contracts typically run for multiple years; vendors such as Digital Realty lean toward this side. In recent years the boundary between the two has blurred: wholesalers are learning to do interconnection, and retailers take large orders too.

The third kind is the equipment vendor, but this kind has to be split in two before it makes sense. Chip and server vendors (NVIDIA, Supermicro, and others) sell the things that iterate fastest: AI computing hardware in particular — GPU platforms and AI servers often turn over a generation in one to two years (general-purpose servers cycle somewhat more slowly, generally three to five years, but still far faster than the mechanical and electrical equipment in the building) — and if inventory is bet wrong, the loss is real money, and the business cycle turns on them faster than on anyone. Power and cooling equipment vendors (UPS units, transformers, busways, diesel generators, precision air conditioners, chillers, liquid-cooling components) run a different business: product life cycles are long, and after a sale there are years of maintenance contracts behind it, so the revenue curve is much smoother. When the trade talks about "the ones selling water" during a gold rush, it usually means this second group — wave after wave of gold-seekers come and go, and someone is always sitting in the water-seller's seat. Lumping the two together and talking about "equipment vendors" as one thing is the most common crudity in looking at this industry.

The fourth kind is the people in the build-and-operate stretch: design institutes, general contractors, mechanical and electrical subcontractors, commissioning teams, and, after completion, the operations and maintenance service providers. Many campus owners do not run operations themselves but outsource them to specialist teams; and a large number of technicians and engineers work on campuses as contract staff — a point especially important for people entering the field, and Chapter 12 of this book, the job map, will deal specifically with this ecosystem in the Bay Area. The "operators" in this chapter's title mean, within this stretch, the party responsible for operation after completion (referred to below collectively as the build-and-operate side). One clarification first: in the industry, colocation providers such as Equinix and Digital Realty are also commonly called "data center operators" in general usage. To avoid confusion, this book places them throughout in the second category, "colocation providers." They are also not the same thing as telecommunications carriers — telecom carriers hold a separate position in the industry chain, selling bandwidth, transmission, and interconnection egress; every fiber that leaves a campus has their business behind it, and Chapter 7 of this book will meet them again when it covers networking.

Money between the four kinds of players does not flow from station to station the way it does on an assembly line. When a hyperscale cloud provider builds its own campus, it skips the colocation provider entirely, orders directly from equipment vendors, and hires its own general contractor; the colocation stretch simply does not exist. When a colocation provider buys mechanical and electrical equipment, it is betting its own capital that this batch of capacity can be rented out over the next ten years. The more accurate way to put it is this: the demand side decides how big the cake gets, and who pays for the equipment and the build — and who carries the risk of idle capacity — depends on whether a given deal goes self-built or colocation. If this account is not clear, jobs become confusing: people working in the same campus may be drawing pay from four or five different companies.

## 2.2 How the Money Is Made

To understand this industry, first understand its unit of account: the megawatt.

The most common pricing method in colocation contracts charges by the power capacity a customer occupies — what is rented is not floor area, it is electricity. How much equipment fits in a rack depends on how many kilowatts that rack is allocated; how much a facility can sell for depends on how many megawatts it has available to sell. That is why the industry says "power is inventory": secure a power allocation and you have secured saleable stock; fail to get the power approved, and a building, however handsome, is an empty shell. This also explains why the protagonists of industry news in recent years have so often been substations and transmission lines rather than servers.

The cost structure turns on electricity in the same way. Of the construction cost, civil works are only one part; mechanical and electrical equipment (power distribution plus cooling) often accounts for the largest share. Of the operating cost, the electricity bill is the largest variable expense, and every small difference in a facility's Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE, covered in detail in Chapter 5), multiplied by megawatts and years, becomes a large sum of money. Labor cost comes after that: the higher a facility's grade, the heavier the staffing for duty shifts and maintenance.

The cloud providers' account is calculated differently. The facility itself does not directly produce revenue — apart from the portion of capacity sold externally as cloud — and the real revenue comes from the cloud services and AI services running inside it. So what they watch in a facility is a few hard lines: speed to launch, with the cycle from groundbreaking to production at a new campus being compressed again and again; cost per megawatt driven to the lowest possible, because at their scale any decimal point is multiplied into an enormous sum; and failures that recover automatically, with as few people watching as possible. In front of them, equipment vendors and colocation providers are the side that gets price-compared over and over.

The accounts of equipment vendors and of the build-and-operate side also need to be set out in full in this section. Mechanical and electrical equipment vendors make two sums of money: first the one-off revenue from the equipment sale, then the maintenance contract — money collected by the year, with promised response times and spare-parts supply; as long as the campus does not stop, their contracts keep renewing. The build side works on construction settlement: payment by milestone, with advance funding and schedule as their two great risks; only when the work is done fast and acceptance passes smoothly does the profit land in the pocket. Operations service providers collect an annual fee for headcount and service, and compete on shift-scheduling efficiency and incident rates.

Put the four sides' accounts together and one rough line emerges: whoever holds what is scarcest at the time has the strongest pricing power. When chips are scarce, the chip vendors call the tune; when power is scarce, the campuses holding power allocations do. But this line needs a discount when it is carried over to individuals: a scarce link in the chain does not mean the jobs in it are valuable. What is valuable is the hard-to-replace skill itself — however tight power allocations are, the jobs running the application paperwork may see no raise at all, because people who can run it are not scarce. The career-planning chapters later in this book will develop this point.

## 2.4 A Bay Area Sample

First, one line on why this sample was chosen: the world's largest concentration of data center capacity is in the Northern Virginia area, and this book does not use it as the sample — first, because the author does not work there, and writing first-hand from the floor beats writing second-hand observation; second, because the Bay Area happens to set out this industry's contradictions most plainly — the densest customers and the tightest power and land, the two forces butting heads on the same ground, give a newcomer the most to look at. Push the lens in to the Silicon Valley Bay Area, where the author works.

The Bay Area's advantages are hard ones: a density of cloud provider and AI company headquarters matched in few places in the world, with customers right next door; a convergence of fiber backbone networks and good interconnection conditions; and an ecosystem of engineers and technicians built up over decades, which makes hiring relatively easy. The Santa Clara area has therefore gathered a large number of data centers, and the clusters of campuses supplied by the municipal power utility are a local sight. (Specific campuses and capacity figures will be added only after they are checked item by item against published sources at final review; this draft writes no figures.)

The Bay Area's constraints are equally hard: land is expensive, grid capacity is tight, new projects wait in line for power for longer and longer, and approvals and community relations also cost money. The result is a split in the industry: part of the demand stays in the Bay Area — mainly the part that is latency-sensitive and must sit close to customers and headquarters — and part spills over to regions with cheap power and low land prices, such as the emerging data center clusters in the U.S. Midwest and Southeast. The site-selection logic has changed from "close to the customer" to "close to the power," the biggest geographical change in the industry in recent years, and Chapter 3 will return to this point when it covers the power bottleneck.

For newcomers in the Bay Area, this sample carries one more meaning: the employer types here are the most complete — cloud providers' own campuses, colocation campuses, equipment vendors' laboratories and service teams, and operations outsourcers are all hiring, and a complete sample of the job ecosystem is right at hand. The job map in Chapter 12 will be written with the Bay Area as its default setting.

## 2.5 Where the Jobs Sit in the Industry Chain

This chapter closes by translating the four kinds of players into jobs, laying a line for Part Four.

On the cloud provider side: facilities engineers, data center technicians, capacity planning, and project managers (TPM) — the highest bar and the heaviest process, offset by a large platform and a strong résumé line. On the colocation side: facilities operations, customer delivery, and interconnection support — a fast pace and contact with many kinds of customers, a good place to train all-round on-site ability. On the equipment vendor side: product engineers (PE), test engineers (TE), and field service engineers — closest to the hardware, where the learning of the manufacturing end lives (the home ground of Chapters 8 and 9 of this book); on the mechanical and electrical equipment vendor side (power distribution, cooling) there are also front-line roles such as field mechanical and electrical commissioning engineers and facility monitoring technicians, whose headcount often exceeds that of the manufacturing end, and who are the home ground of Chapters 4 and 5. On the build-and-operate side: commissioning engineers, operations technicians, and shift leads — demand is large and the entry relatively wide, and it is the first stop for many career changers.

Read this distribution map first, then look at job boards, and the picture is clear: the same title, "data center engineer," can mean completely different work at the four kinds of players — before sending a résumé, identify the player first, then the job. What each job actually does, what certificates it takes, and what it pays are the task of Chapters 12 and 13.
